How to get an apostille in Colorado

Short answer: The Colorado Secretary of State, 1700 Broadway, Suite 550, Denver, charges $5 per apostille when you mail or drop off your documents, and $15 per apostille for expedited walk-in service. Pay by check or money order to “Colorado Secretary of State”; cards aren’t accepted. There’s no online apostille.[source][source]

Since April 2, 2018, Colorado has issued one combined certificate of authentication. For a country in the Hague Apostille Convention, that certificate serves as the apostille.[source] Colorado won’t issue one for use in the United States or its territories (American Samoa, Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands, Puerto Rico, and the U.S. Virgin Islands).[source]

Which documents Colorado can apostille

The original document must carry at least one of these: a Colorado notary public’s signature, a certification by Colorado Vital Records, or a certification by a Colorado county clerk’s office.[source] The rejection guide adds originals certified by the Colorado Secretary of State or the Colorado State Archives.[source] The office doesn’t notarize.[source]

Your documentWhere it comes from
Colorado birth or death certificateA certified copy from the Vital Records Office of Colorado[source]
Marriage license, divorce decree, court documentsCopies from the County Clerk and Recorder or the Clerk of the District Court where they were issued[source]
Business recordsFree certified copies from the Secretary of State’s business database (“Get certified copies of documents” on the entity’s summary page). Print in color for official use.[source]
Power of attorney, school transcripts, other private documentsNotarized by a Colorado notary public[source]
Certificate of NaturalizationNot Colorado’s. Get a certified true copy from the U.S. Department of Homeland Security, then send it to the U.S. Department of State, Office of Authentications.[source]

For business records, check first whether the receiving country accepts a plain certified copy; Colorado says certified copies generally meet other countries’ requirements. It only adds an apostille to business records for Hague countries. For other countries, the certified copies go to the U.S. Department of State.[source]

Out-of-state records: Colorado can’t authenticate another state’s document, but a Colorado notary can make a certified copy of an out-of-state government document such as a birth certificate or marriage license.[source] A Colorado notary can’t certify a copy of a record you can get from a Colorado clerk and recorder, the Secretary of State, the State Archives, or a vital records office; order a certified copy from that office instead.[source]

Why Colorado rejects documents

  • Blanks: every blank must be filled in or crossed out, or the notarization doesn’t comply with Colorado law.[source][source]
  • The notary statement: it needs original signatures and must show the state, the county, and the date of notarization. The document can be in another language, but the notary’s statement must be in English.[source]
  • Powers of attorney with legal conclusions: many foreign POAs have the notary state the parties’ legal capacity or say the notary explained the document. Unless the notary is also a Colorado-licensed attorney, that is the unauthorized practice of law, and Colorado will authenticate the POA only if it is revised to fit Colorado law or the notary is a Colorado attorney. The consulate or embassy may be another option.[source]
  • “Proof of life” certifications: a Colorado notary can’t certify that someone is alive. The person can instead swear to a written statement that they are alive and have that notarized; check that the receiving country accepts it.[source]
  • I-9 forms: they aren’t notarized and are federal documents.[source]
  • Certain subjects: Colorado won’t authenticate notarized records about allegiance, renouncing or relinquishing citizenship, sovereignty, “in itinere” status, the World Service Authority, or claimed immunity from state or federal law.[source]

Free review first: email a PDF of your document to Authentications@coloradosos.gov and the office will review it and give feedback, within 3 to 5 business days according to its rejection guide.[source][source] If you plan to notarize online, the notary must be a Colorado notary public for Colorado to authenticate it, so the review is a good way to check the certificate wording first.[source]

What to send

  1. The original document.[source]
  2. The completed Apostilles & Authentications Request Form (a Spanish version is on the program page), naming the country where the documents will be used and the number of apostilles.[source][source]
  3. A check or money order to “Colorado Secretary of State” for $5 per apostille.[source]
  4. Your return choice: hold for pickup, free standard USPS, or a prepaid FedEx or UPS label. Don’t put the office’s own name or address as the return address on the label, or the documents come back by standard mail.[source]

Receiving an apostille issued in Colorado? You can verify it on the Secretary of State’s site.[source] For the destination country, see apostille by country; for non-Hague countries, see apostille versus authentication.
