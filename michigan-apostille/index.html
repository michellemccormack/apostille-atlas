How to get an apostille in Michigan

Short answer: Michigan apostilles come from the Office of the Great Seal, part of the Michigan Department of State. The fee is $1 per document, paid by check or money order to “State of Michigan.” You can mail the request to Lansing or book an office visit. There is no online filing and no expedited service.[source][source]

Michigan issues an apostille for countries in the Hague Apostille Convention and a Certificate of Authority for every other country. Both have the same requirements, fee, and instructions. Michigan won’t issue either one for a document that will be used in the United States.[source]

Which offices can do it in person?

Document authentication is available, by appointment, at the Office of the Great Seal (Richard H. Austin Building, 1st Floor, 430 W. Allegan Street, Lansing) and at eight Secretary of State offices: Clinton Township, Detroit (West Grand Boulevard), Flint (Fenton Road), Grand Rapids (28th Street), Lansing (East Michigan Avenue), Livonia, Marquette, and Pontiac.[source][source] Book through the Secretary of State appointment system. Bring the signed request form, the $1 per document fee, and the documents.[source]

Step by step by mail

  1. Get the right version of the document. Use the table below. Vital records must be certified copies, and anything in a foreign language needs a written English translation.[source]
  2. Fill out and sign the Authentication Request Form. It asks for your name, phone and email, the return address, the number of documents, and the destination country. For China it also asks whether the document is going to Mainland China, Hong Kong, or Macao.[source]
  3. Add the fee: a check or money order for $1 per document to the State of Michigan.[source]
  4. Add a return envelope: a self-addressed, stamped envelope or a prepaid air bill (FedEx, UPS, USPS). On a courier air bill, list yourself as both sender and receiver.[source] The office won’t pay return postage.[source]
  5. Mail everything to 7064 Crowner Drive, Lansing, MI 48918.[source]

Document rules

DocumentMichigan rule
Birth, death, marriage, divorce recordsOnly certified copies from the county clerk, the State Registrar, or (for Wayne, Oakland, and Macomb counties only) the city clerk. Not copies from court or probate clerks, not notarized vital records, not photocopies, and not records from other states. Don’t notarize the certificate or attach anything to it.[source][source] The Great Seal’s instruction sheet also says the record must have been produced within 15 years.[source] You can ask the State Registrar (517-335-8666) for a certified copy for use abroad and arrange for it to be mailed straight to the Office of the Great Seal.[source] Certified copies are ordered through MDHHS Vital Records.[source]
Michigan court documentsMust be a Michigan court document. If the copy is certified by the court and a court official, the person named in it adds a signed “true copy” statement in front of a Michigan notary.[source]
Diplomas and transcriptsOn your original, write or type “this is my original (name of record)” and sign it in front of a Michigan notary. On a copy, write “this is a true copy of my (name of record)” and sign it the same way. A school official can instead state “this is a true copy,” add their title, and sign in front of a Michigan notary (no signature stamps). The authentication is permanently attached, so you may prefer to use a copy.[source]
Criminal background checksMichigan checks come from the Michigan State Police (including the I-CHAT online tool). If the agency didn’t notarize the record, put a true copy statement on it and sign it before a Michigan notary.[source]
Power of attorney and other signed papersSigned in front of a Michigan notary, with original signatures. The notary must not be named at the beginning of the document as if attesting to the whole thing, and the notarial block can’t include details about the signer such as address or date of birth. Foreign-language documents need a notarized English translation.[source]
Articles of incorporationCertified copies issued by the Michigan Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs (LARA).[source]

Why a Michigan notary, and what is a “true copy” statement?

Michigan notaries can’t certify that a copy is a true copy. Only the person named on the document (or the person or institution that produced it) can make that statement. The notary then notarizes that person’s signature.[source] A parent can sign for someone under 18.[source]

The Office of the Great Seal checks the notarization closely. It must show the notary’s printed name, signature, date, commission expiration date, county of commission, and the county of the act if different. An embosser alone isn’t enough, the whole notarial block must be on one page, and stamped or photocopied notary signatures are rejected.[source][source][source]

Documents notarized by an out-of-state notary can’t be authenticated in Michigan.[source] If you plan to notarize online, the notary still has to be a Michigan notary for Michigan to apostille it.

What Michigan won’t authenticate

  • Documents notarized by an out-of-state notary, and out-of-country or out-of-state records and vital records.[source]
  • Business and non-business documents with no tie to Michigan.[source]
  • U.S. immigration and naturalization paperwork.[source]
  • Documents to be used in the United States.[source]
  • Documents that make claims about citizenship, immunity, allegiance, or sovereignty, or that use “Notario Publico” or “Notaria Publica.”[source]

Non-Hague countries

For a country outside the Hague Convention, Michigan issues a Certificate of Authority. The Great Seal notes that many of these countries only accept documents certified at both the state and federal levels, so expect a further step at the U.S. Department of State and possibly the embassy.[source] See apostille versus authentication and apostille by country.
