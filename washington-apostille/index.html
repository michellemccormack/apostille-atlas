How to get an apostille in Washington State

Short answer: The Washington Secretary of State charges $15 per document. Standard mail service typically takes 7 to 10 business days; expedited service adds $100 per 10 documents and typically takes 2 to 3 business days; same-day service in person at Tumwater or Cheney adds $150 for the first 10 documents. [source][source]

The Corporations & Charities Division of the Washington Secretary of State certifies documents signed by a Washington state official or a Washington notary public for use in another country. Washington issues one “universal certificate” that works as an apostille for Hague Convention countries or as an authentication for other countries.[source]

Fees and times as published on the apostille resource page and the fee schedule.[source][source] By mail, pay by check or money order in U.S. dollars to “Secretary of State.”[source][source] The division says payments are not refundable.[source]

Step by step

  1. Check the document is eligible. It must be an original or certified copy issued by a Washington government entity, a certified Washington court document, or a personal document notarized by a Washington notary with a full notarial certificate.[source]
  2. Fill out the Apostille Authentication Request Form. It asks for the country requesting the document, your name, address, email, and phone, and the number of documents.[source]
  3. Choose standard or expedited and add the fee.[source]
  4. Mail it, courier it, or bring it in using the addresses above.[source]

Document rules

DocumentWashington rule
Birth, death, marriage, divorce certificates; letter of single statusSend an original certified copy. Vital records must be apostilled by the state that issued them, so a birth certificate from another state goes to that state.[source] Certified copies come from the county auditor that issued the marriage certificate or the Center for Vital Statistics in Tumwater.[source]
Public high school transcriptsNo notarization, but they must be official transcripts signed by the principal, vice principal, or registrar (a district superintendent or assistant superintendent also works). Ask the school for the signature specifically.[source]
Washington college and university recordsOfficial transcripts signed by the registrar (associate or interim registrar also accepted); original degrees are signed by the president. Both must carry the school seal.[source] An original diploma doesn’t need a notary.[source]
Private and out-of-state school documentsNotarized by a Washington notary. Copies of out-of-state school documents are accepted only when notarized by a Washington notary.[source][source]
Personal documents (power of attorney, affidavits, consents)Properly notarized by a Washington notary.[source]
Washington background checkA notarized clearance letter from the Washington State Patrol. If the check was fingerprint-based, include the original fingerprint card.[source]
Business entity recordsOnly certified copies or certificates of documents on file with the Corporations Division. Order the certified copy and the apostille together on the Business Records Request form; these can’t be ordered online.[source]
Driver license or passportA notarized copy. Outside Washington, you can send an Abstract Driving Record from the Department of Licensing, issued within 30 days of reaching the office.[source][source]
FBI checks, naturalization certificates, military-notary documentsFederal documents; the Secretary of State can’t apostille them. They go to the U.S. Department of State.[source][U.S. State Dept.]

What makes a notarization acceptable?

The notary certificate must be complete, signed, and stamped by a Washington notary, with the notary’s signed and printed name, title, commission expiration date, the date of the notarial act, and the Washington state and county where it took place. The signature name and stamp name must match each other and the Notary Division license directory. A notary signature without a notarial certificate can’t be authenticated.[source]

Since 2016, the Secretary of State won’t certify a notary’s signature on documents about allegiance to a government, renouncing citizenship or military status, or claiming immunity from state or federal law.[source]

Non-Hague countries and verification

For a country that isn’t in the Hague Convention, the universal certificate may need further certification by the U.S. Department of State, the country’s embassy, or both.[source] See apostille versus authentication and apostille by country. The Secretary of State also offers an online apostille and certificate authenticity search.[source]
