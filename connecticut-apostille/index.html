How to get an apostille in Connecticut

Short answer: Order online through the Connecticut Secretary of the State’s apostille system; paper order forms are no longer accepted. For a paper apostille you then mail or courier the original to Hartford, and regular orders are processed within 5 to 7 business days of arrival. Eligible documents can get an e-Apostille instead, processed within 24 hours.[source] The fee set by law is $40 per document, or $15 for documents connected with a child’s adoption, and an e-Apostille costs the same as paper.[source]

The Secretary of the State issues an apostille for Hague Apostille Convention countries and an authentication for other countries, which may then need the U.S. Department of State or the destination country’s embassy. Since July 21, 2025, Connecticut uses a single authentication certificate for all legalization requests.[source]

Fees are from the Office of Legislative Research summary of Public Act 26-81, which sets the per-document fee for apostilles and authentications.[source] The online system shows your total when you order.[portal] Postage and courier costs are yours.

Step by step

  1. Prepare the document. Use an original or a certified copy. It must be notarized by a Connecticut notary or certified by a Connecticut official, within the last 10 years, with an original signature and/or seal.[source]
  2. Order online. Go to sots.service.ct.gov/apostille, create an account, log in, and place the order. All orders must be submitted online.[source]
  3. e-Apostille: upload the document to be processed digitally.[source]
  4. Paper apostille: mail the original or certified document with your printed order receipt (or your APO Work Order number) and a prepaid, pre-addressed return envelope or shipping label.[source]
  5. Track it in your online account, which has real-time order tracking and secure messaging.[source]

Connecticut began issuing e-Apostilles on July 6, 2026, and not every document type is eligible yet. e-Apostilles are accepted by Hague Convention members, but the office suggests confirming with the receiving agency or institution before you order. Anyone can check a Connecticut paper apostille or e-Apostille on the verification portal.[source]

What Connecticut can authenticate

Only documents signed by a Connecticut public official: Connecticut notaries public; town clerks and their assistants and deputies; local registrars of vital statistics and their assistants and deputies; clerks and assistant clerks of the Superior and Probate Courts; commissioners of Connecticut state agencies; and justices of the peace.[source]

DocumentConnecticut rule
Birth, death, marriage certificatesA certified copy from the town clerk or the Department of Public Health Vital Records Unit, with a paper apostille.[source] See how to obtain a record.
Copies of documentsA copy can be authenticated if it comes with a written statement that it is true and accurate, sworn to and signed before a Connecticut notary.[source]
Court recordsSigned by a clerk or assistant clerk of the Superior or Probate Court. Out-of-state court documents go to the issuing state.[source]
School transcriptsConnecticut schools only; out-of-state transcripts are authenticated by the state where the school is.[source]
Federal documents (for example, FBI checks)Not Connecticut’s; they go to the U.S. Department of State.[source]

What Connecticut won’t authenticate

  • Documents signed by an out-of-state notary.[source]
  • Documents executed by an attorney signing as a Commissioner of the Superior Court.[source]
  • Out-of-state vital records, court documents, and school transcripts.[source]
  • Documents to be presented in a U.S. territory such as Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa, or the Northern Mariana Islands. Territories aren’t foreign jurisdictions, so a properly notarized or Connecticut-issued document can be used there without a certificate.[source]

Getting the notarization right

Because the notary must be commissioned in Connecticut, a notarization by a notary from another state won’t work.[source] Connecticut notaries may charge up to $10 per notarial act since July 1, 2026, and Connecticut has allowed remote notarial acts since October 1, 2023.[source] If you use online notarization, make sure the notary holds a Connecticut commission.
