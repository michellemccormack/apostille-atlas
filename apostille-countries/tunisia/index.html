Apostille for Tunisia

Tunisia is a Contracting Party to the 1961 Hague Apostille Convention, in force there since March 30, 2018.[source] A U.S. public document headed to Tunisia generally needs an apostille from the U.S. office that covers the document, not embassy legalization.

Objections to Tunisia’s accession

When a country accedes, existing Parties have six months to object, and the Convention then does not apply between the two. The HCCH records the following for Tunisia:

Germany 01-12-2017 (Translation) [...] the Federal Republic of Germany herewith objects to the accession of Tunisia in accordance with article 12, paragraph 2 of the Convention Abolishing the Requirements of Legalization for Foreign Documents (The Hague, October 5th, 1961). Austria 12-01-2018 The Republic of Austria raises an objection to the accession of the Tunisian Republic with reference to Article 12, paragraph 2, of the Convention. Greece 25-01-2018 [...] in accordance with Article 12, paragraph 2 of the Hague Convention abolishing the requirement of legalization for foreign public documents of 5 October 1961, the Hellenic Republic hereby objects to the accession of the Republic of Tunisia to the above mentioned Convention. Belgium 26-01-2018 (Translation) [...] Belgium objects to the accession of Tunisia to the above mentioned Convention in accordance with Article 12 of the Convention. 11-06-2025 (translation) (original: Dutch) On 26 January 2018 Belgium raised an objection to the accession of Tunisia to the abovementioned Convention in accordance with article 12 of the Convention, as a result of which the Convention did not enter into force between Tunisia and Belgium. […] the Kingdom of Belgium has the honor to inform the […] depositary of the Apostille Convention of the withdrawal of this objection. …[source]

Sending a U.S. document to Tunisia

The apostille comes from the U.S. jurisdiction that issued or notarized the document, not from Tunisia. Under the U.S. designation filed with the Hague Conference:[source]

  • State documents (birth, marriage, and death certificates, state court records, documents signed by a notary in that state): the competent authority of that state, generally the Secretary of State. See who issues apostilles in each state.
  • Federal documents (documents issued by U.S. federal agencies): the U.S. Department of State Office of Authentications.
  • U.S. federal court documents: the clerk or a deputy clerk of the federal court that issued them.
  • Consular Reports of Birth, Death, and Marriage Abroad: the State Department’s Passport Services, Vital Records Section.

The office in Tunisia that receives your document can still set its own rules about translations, how recent the document must be, and whether it accepts a notarized copy. Ask before you order the apostille.
