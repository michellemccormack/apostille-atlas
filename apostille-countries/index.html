Apostille by country

The HCCH status table, checked November 23, 2026, lists 130 Contracting Parties to the 1961 Apostille Convention, including the United States.[source] Each country below has its own page with its entry-into-force date, whether the Convention applies with the U.S., and the authority that issues apostilles there.

If the destination country is on this list and the Convention is in force, a U.S. document generally needs an apostille. If it is not on the list, plan on authentication plus embassy or consulate legalization. How the two differ.

Common destinations that are not on the list

These countries and territories did not appear in the HCCH status table on November 23, 2026, so an apostille alone is not the usual route for them:[source]

Afghanistan, Angola, Cambodia, Cameroon, Cuba, Egypt, Ethiopia, Ghana, Haiti, Iran, Iraq, Jordan, Kenya, Kuwait, Lebanon, Libya, Malaysia, Myanmar, Nepal, Nigeria, Qatar, Sri Lanka, Sudan, Syria, Taiwan, Tanzania, Uganda, United Arab Emirates, Yemen, Zambia, Zimbabwe.

For these destinations the chain is usually a state authentication certificate, sometimes a further certificate from the U.S. Department of State, and then legalization by the destination country’s embassy or consulate in the U.S. Each state’s rules differ; see apostille versus authentication and the state offices list.
