How to verify an apostille

Short answer: Only the office that issued the apostille can verify it. Look at the apostille for the issuing authority, the date and the certificate number, then search that office’s online register (e-Register) if it has one, or contact the office and ask it to check its register. The Hague Conference itself doesn’t issue, record or verify apostilles.[source][source]

Every apostille authority has to keep a register of the apostilles it issues, and at the request of anyone interested it must confirm whether the details on an apostille match that register. You don’t have to explain why you’re asking.[source] Seventeen U.S. authorities have a public online register listed by the Hague Conference.[source]

Step by step

  1. Read the apostille. The certificate names who signed the underlying document and in what capacity (or, for an unsigned document, the authority whose seal is on it), the date of the apostille, the authority that issued it, and its number. Those are the details every register must record.[source]
  2. Find the issuing office. For a U.S. apostille that is the state office named on the certificate (in most states the Secretary of State), or the U.S. Department of State for a federal document.[source] Our apostille offices by state table lists every designated office.
  3. Use the e-Register if there is one. If the authority runs an e-Register, its web address is printed on the apostille.[source] The U.S. registers listed by the Hague Conference are in the table below.
  4. Otherwise, ask the office. Where there is no e-Register, send the request to the authority that issued the apostille and it checks its register manually.[source] Contact details for every competent authority are on the Hague Conference’s authorities pages.[source]

Some registers cover only apostilles issued after a certain date, and a country may run separate registers for separate authorities.[source] New York, for example, says its online tool covers apostilles and certificates issued on or after April 9, 2013.[source] A state that isn’t on the list, such as Arizona or Georgia, can still verify its apostilles on request.[source]

e-Apostilles

An e-Apostille is an apostille issued and signed in digital form. The Hague Conference says it can’t be refused just because it is electronic and is as valid as a paper apostille in every Contracting Party.[source] e-Apostilles issued during the state pilot programs in the table are legitimate, validly issued e-Apostilles, not test certificates.[source] An e-Register can record paper apostilles and e-Apostilles alike.[source]

What a match does and doesn’t tell you

A matching register entry confirms the apostille was issued as shown. It doesn’t vouch for what the document says: an apostille only certifies the origin of the public document, meaning the signature, the capacity of the signer, and any seal or stamp.[source] The receiving country still decides whether the document itself is acceptable, for example whether it meets its domestic requirements or has been altered.[source] An apostille also can’t be turned down just because it is old; see does an apostille expire?
