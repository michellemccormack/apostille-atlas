Apostille versus authentication

Short answer: Use an apostille when the destination country is a party to the 1961 Hague Apostille Convention; one certificate replaces the longer legalization chain. For other countries, U.S. states usually issue an authentication or certification instead, and the U.S. Department of State or the country's embassy or consulate may need to add a further step. [source]

The paper you need depends on the destination country, and on which U.S. office issued or notarized the document. States do not all use the same certificate for non-Hague countries.

To see which certificate your document needs and the steps for your state, use the apostille finder.

What is an apostille?

The Convention of 5 October 1961 Abolishing the Requirement of Legalisation for Foreign Public Documents — the HCCH 1961 Apostille Convention — is meant to replace the traditional, often long legalization chain with a single apostille issued by a competent authority in the place where the document originates.[source] The HCCH Apostille Section, checked November 22, 2026, stated that the Convention has 130 Contracting Parties.[source]

The current list of Contracting Parties is maintained by the Hague Conference. We do not reproduce that list here because it changes when countries join. Check the official HCCH status table for the country that will receive the document.

The HCCH also notes that only designated competent authorities of Contracting Parties may issue or verify apostilles, and that an e-Apostille (an apostille issued in digital form) cannot be refused simply because it is electronic.[source] Whether a particular U.S. state issues e-apostilles is a separate question; none of the five state pages reviewed for this site advertised an e-apostille option on the date we checked.

What is an authentication certificate?

When the destination country is not a party to the Apostille Convention, U.S. offices often issue a different certificate, sometimes called a certificate of authentication or a notarial certification. That certificate may not finish the process: the destination country may still require legalization by its embassy or consulate, and some U.S. states say a further certification from the U.S. Department of State may be required first.

For federal documents, and for any extra State Department step after a state authentication, use the official pages: Authenticate Your Document and the Office of Authentications.

Which certificate does each state issue?

StateWhat the official page says
New YorkAn apostille is issued for use in countries that have joined the 1961 Hague Convention. A Certificate of Authentication is issued for use in a country that is not a member. Certificates of Authentication “may also require an additional certification from the United States Department of State prior to submission to the foreign country.”[source]
CaliforniaThe California Secretary of State “issues a single Apostille for documents to be used outside of the United States, regardless of the country of destination.” It does not issue a separate authentication certificate.[source]
TexasTexas issues a “universal Apostille.” It “operates as an apostille for countries that are party to the Apostille Convention and operates as an authentication for countries that are not.” If the destination country is not a party, Texas says you “likely need to get your universal Apostille validated by the Office of Authentications with the U.S. State Department.”[source]
FloridaFlorida issues an apostille for Hague Convention member states. “Countries that are nonmembers of the 1961 Hague Convention are not issued an apostille; they are issued notarial certificates or certifications.”[source]
Massachusetts“If the country is a signatory to the Hague Convention, the document will be called an apostille.” The office issues certifications and apostilles, and the official page tells you to identify the destination country because “different countries require different types of certification.”[source]
New JerseyThe Division of Revenue and Enterprise Services provides an apostille if the country subscribes to the Hague Convention. If the country is not on the Hague list, it provides a certification of the public official.[source]
Virginia“Depending on the destination country, the authentication is issued either as a Great Seal or an Apostille.”[source]
North CarolinaThree certificates: an Apostille for Hague countries; an Authentication for North Carolina government-issued documents going to non-Hague countries; and an Authority for documents notarized in North Carolina going to non-Hague countries.[source]
PennsylvaniaThe destination country determines whether the authentication is an Apostille or a Certification. Pennsylvania now prints both on a single universal certificate; a certification may still need the U.S. Department of State and the foreign embassy.[source]
MissouriA certification verifies a notary; an authentication verifies recorders of deeds, circuit clerks, judges, county clerks, and the State Registrar; an apostille is issued for Hague Convention countries.[source]
WashingtonOne “universal certificate” serves as an Apostille for Hague countries or an Authentication for non-member countries, which may also need the U.S. Department of State, the embassy, or both.[source]
MichiganAn Apostille for Hague countries and a Certificate of Authority for all others, with the same requirements and fee. Many non-Hague countries also want a federal-level certification.[source]
ConnecticutAn Apostille for Hague countries and an Authentication for others, which may need further legalization by the U.S. Department of State or the embassy. Connecticut also issues e-Apostilles for eligible documents, but not for vital records.[source]
MarylandAn Apostille for Hague countries and a standard certification for others; a standard certification then goes to the U.S. Department of State before the embassy.[source]
GeorgiaTwo agencies: the GSCCCA issues apostilles for Hague countries; the Secretary of State issues a Great Seal Certification for all others.[source]
ColoradoOne combined certificate of authentication since April 2, 2018, which serves as an apostille in Hague countries.[source]
MinnesotaOnly an apostille, whatever the destination. If the country doesn’t accept apostilles, contact its embassy or consulate about options.[source]
ArizonaAn Apostille or a certificate of Authentication, depending on the country that requested it. Arizona issues paper certificates only, no electronic apostilles.[source]

Who authenticates federal and out-of-state documents?

State apostille offices generally authenticate only that state’s public documents or documents notarized by that state’s notaries. New York says Federal/FBI background checks must be authenticated by the U.S. Department of State.[source] Florida says federal documents should be submitted to the U.S. Department of State, and that a document issued or notarized in another state should go to that state’s competent authority.[source] Massachusetts says it does not certify out-of-state documents; they must be certified by the state of origin.[source]

The federal fee and the current Office of Authentications process are not quoted here. Use the official State Department pages linked above.
